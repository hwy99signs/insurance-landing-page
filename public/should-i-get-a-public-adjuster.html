Should I Get a Public Adjuster?

The honest answer — what a public adjuster is, the whole process, how they are paid, and the real pros and cons.

What a public adjuster is

A public adjuster is a licensed professional that you hire to represent your side of an insurance claim. That is what makes them different from the adjuster your insurance company sends: the company's adjuster works for the insurance company. A public adjuster works for the policyholder.

Public adjusters are licensed and regulated by your state's Department of Insurance. ICG is not a public adjuster and does not negotiate claims — this page is education, so you can decide for yourself with clear eyes.

The whole process, step by step

  • 1. Review. The public adjuster reviews your policy and your damage, usually free at the start, and tells you whether they think they can help.
  • 2. Contract. If you hire them, you sign a contract stating their fee — read it fully before you sign (see "How they are paid" below).
  • 3. Documentation. They inspect and document the damage in detail — photos, measurements, and a full estimate of what repairs should cost.
  • 4. Presentation. They prepare and present your claim to the insurance company on your behalf.
  • 5. Negotiation. They handle the back-and-forth with the insurer's adjuster and argue for the settlement they believe your policy supports.
  • 6. Settlement. When the claim settles, their fee is taken as the agreed percentage of the settlement.

How they are paid

Almost always a contingency fee: a percentage of the amount the insurance company pays on your claim. Percentages vary by state, by claim size, and by firm; some states cap the percentage by law, and many states set a lower cap for claims from declared disasters. There is usually no upfront cost — but the percentage comes out of your settlement money, which is exactly why the size and difficulty of the claim matter so much in this decision.

The same claim, two ways — in real numbers

Take one claim with a $55,000 repair estimate and a $5,000 deductible:

Deductible− $5,000.00
Insurance pays you$50,000.00
Your out of pocket$5,000.00 (the deductible)

With a public adjuster at 10% of the claim total

Claim total$55,000.00
Deductible− $5,000.00
Insurance payment$50,000.00
Public adjuster fee
10% of the $55,000 claim total		− $5,500.00
You receive$44,500.00
Your out of pocket$10,500.00 (deductible + the fee)

Same claim, same $55,000 of repairs. Without a public adjuster you receive $50,000; with one, on a claim that settles for the same amount, you receive $44,500. That is the honest math — a public adjuster earns their fee only when they increase the settlement by more than the fee costs you, which is why the size and difficulty of the claim matter so much in this decision.

Two contract details to check before you sign: in this example the fee is 10% of the claim total — some contracts charge the percentage only on the amount above what the insurer had already offered, so ask which basis your contract uses, in writing. And if supplemental payments are won later, the public adjuster is normally entitled to their percentage of those as well. Neither figure above includes supplements. In Texas, a public adjuster's fee is capped by law at 10% of the settlement amount.

The honest pros

  • Experience you may not have: they read policies and estimates every day.
  • A documented, professionally prepared claim — especially valuable on large or complicated losses.
  • Time and stress off your shoulders during a hard period.
  • On a large, complex, or disputed claim, a good public adjuster can make a real difference to the outcome.

The honest cons

  • The fee is real money out of your settlement — on a small claim, the percentage can eat most of any improvement.
  • The process can take longer with a third party in the middle.
  • Quality varies widely. So does honesty. Licensing and references matter.
  • For a normal, common damage claim that is moving along fairly, you may be paying for help you did not need.

When it is usually worth it

  • Very large claims — major fire, extensive water damage, a roof and interior together.
  • Complex claims — multiple causes of loss, commercial property, business interruption.
  • Disputed, denied, or clearly underpaid claims where you have hit a wall.
  • When you truly cannot manage the claim yourself because of health, work, or distance.

When it usually is not

  • Normal, common damage claims — a straightforward repair the insurer is handling fairly.
  • Small claims, where the fee percentage leaves you with less, not more.
  • Claims that are already documented, agreed, and simply waiting on payment.

Red flags

  • Pressure to sign on the spot, especially right after a storm.
  • Promises of a specific dollar outcome — nobody honest can guarantee that.
  • Anyone who cannot show you a state license, or who asks you to sign a blank or incomplete contract.
  • Anyone who suggests inflating the claim. That is fraud, and it is your name on the claim.

Verify the license — always

Before you sign anything, confirm the license with your state's Department of Insurance. In Texas, that is the Texas Department of Insurance (TDI) at tdi.texas.gov. Every state has an equivalent lookup — it takes five minutes and it is the cheapest protection you will ever buy.

Your other options

  • Handle it yourself, educated. Most normal claims can be managed by an informed policyholder. That is what The Insurance Claims Guide books and the AI Claim Companion exist for.
  • An attorney. For legal disputes — bad faith, coverage litigation — that is a lawyer's work, not an adjuster's.
  • Your state Department of Insurance. Filing a complaint is free, and regulators track every one.
